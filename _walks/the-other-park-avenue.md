faq:
  - q: Is this the famous Park Avenue?
    a: >-
      Same avenue, other end. The doorman-building stretch ends at 96th
      Street, where the Metro-North tracks come out of their tunnel. From
      there north through East Harlem, Park Avenue is the street under the
      railroad — a stone embankment, then a steel viaduct, with markets,
      garden centers and housing beneath and beside it.
  - q: Why do trains run above Park Avenue in Harlem?
    a: >-
      The tracks are Metro-North's main line into Grand Central, descended
      from the New York & Harlem Railroad of the 1830s. Downtown the tracks
      were buried in the 1870s and covered with the landscaped malls that
      gave Park Avenue its name; in East Harlem they stayed above ground on
      an 1875 stone wall and an 1897 steel viaduct that carries roughly 750
      trains a day.
  - q: What is La Marqueta?
    a: >-
      The public market under the viaduct between 111th and 116th Streets,
      opened in 1936 under Mayor LaGuardia to bring East Harlem's pushcart
      vendors indoors. It peaked at over 500 vendors in the 1950s and still
      operates today as a city-run market.
  - q: What is being built along the viaduct?
    a: >-
      The MTA is replacing the 1890s Park Avenue Viaduct section by section
      while trains keep running, using gantry cranes to swap in
      prefabricated deck units. The 115th–123rd Street phase finished its
      structural work in 2025; the 127th–132nd Street phase runs into 2027.

## The journey

Park Avenue is a railroad wearing a fancy name. Downtown, the tracks were
buried and roofed with gardens in the 1870s — that's where the "Park" came
from. At 97th Street they surface, and this walk begins just above, at
**East 100th Street**, beside the **1875 cut-stone embankment** whose
rounded arches swallow the cross streets one by one.

The wall runs shoulder-high with the neighborhood for ten blocks — past
**Tito Puente Way** at 110th, where the mambo king grew up — then gives
way to the **1897 steel viaduct**, and the street underneath fills with
life: the stalls of **La Marqueta**, the public market that has traded
under the tracks since 1936, and at **116th Street** the gold-ringed
medallions of **"Harlem Melodic Moments"**, MTA-commissioned art on the
freshly rebuilt spans, celebrating salsa, jazz, gospel and hip-hop.

At **125th Street** the elevated **Harlem–125th Street station** straddles
the avenue — the only stop besides Grand Central served by every
Metro-North line. Beyond it the walk enters rawer blocks: auto shops,
depots, construction fencing and the huge yellow **gantry cranes**
rebuilding the viaduct deck by deck, new apartment buildings rising in
between. At **East 132nd Street** the avenue quietly ends and the rails
carry on without it, over the **Harlem River Lift Bridge** toward the
Bronx.
